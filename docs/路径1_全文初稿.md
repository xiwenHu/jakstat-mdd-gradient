# The cortical principal gradient accounts for reported transcriptomic–imaging convergence of JAK-STAT signaling in major depressive disorder: a multi-modal evaluation with positive controls

**Running title**: Principal gradient explains transcriptomic–imaging convergence in MDD

**Keywords**: imaging transcriptomics; Allen Human Brain Atlas; cortical thickness; major depressive disorder; JAK-STAT; spatial autocorrelation; null models; Mendelian randomization; Bayes factor

---

## Abstract

**Background** Signalling through JAK/STAT kinases is a leading candidate mechanism linking inflammation to major depressive disorder (MDD), supported by peripheral, cerebrospinal-fluid and preclinical data, and now entering interventional trials. Direct multimodal evidence connecting this pathway to human brain phenotypes is lacking, and imaging-transcriptomic approaches have been widely used to bridge the gap. We asked whether JAK-STAT signalling shows convergent spatial, genetic and postmortem transcriptional associations with MDD-related cortical alterations, and whether any such convergence is distinguishable from cortical spatial structure.

**Methods** We analysed the spatial association between expression of 13 a-priori JAK-STAT genes (Allen Human Brain Atlas; Schaefer-400) and ENIGMA MDD cortical-thickness effect sizes; tested it against 10,000 random gene sets matched on brain expression level, gene length and SNP count; and quantified its dependence on cortical co-expression gradients (PC1–PC5), with Bayes factors. We further tested cell-type attribution, competitive gene-set analysis (MAGMA), cis-Mendelian randomization of *IL6R* (rs2228145 Asp358Ala) against depression and cortical thickness, and differential expression in postmortem brain (GSE53987), with schizophrenia as a positive control.

**Results** The nominal spatial association (ρ = 0.315, *P* = 3.1 × 10⁻⁹; BF₁₀ > 10⁶) was not distinguishable from matched random gene sets (empirical *P* = 0.985/0.978), was negative by competitive gene-set testing (*P* = 0.744), and was absorbed by cortical co-expression gradients (ρ = 0.005 after PC1–PC5; **BF₀₁ = 16.4**, strong evidence for the null). Cell-type attribution pointed to endothelium rather than microglia. *IL6R* instruments showed no effect on depression (OR = 0.989, *P* = 0.230) or global cortical thickness (β = −0.012 SD/allele, *P* = 0.135); Bayes factors were inconclusive to anecdotal (BF₀₁ = 0.8–3.3), consistent with power at 53% of the minimum detectable effect. No JAK-STAT gene was differentially expressed in MDD postmortem brain (0/39 FDR-significant; maximum change 1.10-fold; median BF₀₁ = 4.8), whereas the identical pipeline recovered three genes in schizophrenia (FDR < 0.05; BF₁₀ up to 33).

**Conclusions** Reported transcriptomic–imaging convergence for JAK-STAT signalling in MDD is attributable to cortical spatial architecture rather than pathway-specific biology. We provide a minimum-reporting framework for imaging-transcriptomic enrichment claims.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The hypothesis is active, and therapeutically consequential

Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) signalling is among the most actively pursued candidate mechanisms linking inflammation to major depressive disorder (MDD). Peripheral studies report elevated interleukin-6 (IL-6) and altered STAT3 levels in depression; preclinical work implicates IL-6/STAT3 control of serotonin transporter function and of dopamine signalling in corticostriatal reward circuits; and cerebrospinal-fluid proteomics nominates IL6 and STAT3 among the top predicted upstream regulators of the depressed proteome [1–5]. The hypothesis is no longer purely academic: a biomarker-driven trial is testing the brain-penetrant JAK inhibitor baricitinib for inflammation-related anhedonia and psychomotor slowing in MDD [6]. If JAK-STAT signalling is a causal mechanism of depression-related brain alterations, it should be traceable not only in peripheral biofluids but in the spatial, genetic and transcriptional architecture of the brain itself.

### 1.2 Direct multimodal evidence is lacking — and imaging transcriptomics is the standard bridge

Direct multimodal evidence linking JAK-STAT pathway alterations to neuroimaging phenotypes in MDD, however, remains lacking. As a recent integrative review put it, existing neuroimaging studies of inflammation "do not establish that the observed network abnormalities are specifically related to JAK/STAT signalling … and should be interpreted as converging evidence rather than as demonstrating a direct JAK/STAT–brain relationship" [7]. Imaging transcriptomics — spatial correlation of brain-wide gene expression from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) with disorder-related imaging maps — has become the standard tool for bridging molecular hypotheses and macroscale neuroanatomy. Applied to MDD, it has linked cortical thickness, morphometric similarity, brain ageing and polygenic transcriptional scores to glial, synaptic, metabolic and immune signatures [8–13]. These studies are widely read as molecular validation of candidate pathways.

### 1.3 But the inferential structure of imaging transcriptomics is fragile

Three properties of the design are now well documented. First, regional gene expression and imaging effect maps share strong spatial autocorrelation, and null-model comparisons show inflated type-I error for smooth maps when this is imperfectly controlled [14,15]. Second, a dominant sensory-to-association co-expression gradient (the cortical principal gradient) is expressed across thousands of genes and itself tracks cortical thickness [16,17]; spin tests control spatial autocorrelation but do not remove this shared prior. Third, co-expression means that arbitrary gene sets can appear "enriched": a worked example in EEG genetics showed that a spin-significant, surrogate-corroborated, mechanistically plausible enrichment dissolved against random gene sets matched on co-expression, concluding that correcting for spatial autocorrelation is "necessary but not sufficient" [18]. Spatial interpolation of AHBA data can further inflate false positives [19], and imaging transcriptomics is "susceptible to identifying genes whose co-location with MRI phenotypes reflects secondary associations" [20]. What has been missing is a worked example in a major psychiatric phenotype — with a mechanistic account of *why* the illusion is persuasive, and an independent multi-modal battery that does not share the spatial confound.

### 1.4 This study

Here we evaluate the JAK-STAT hypothesis in MDD with a seven-layer battery designed to separate pathway-specific biology from cortical spatial structure: (i) spatial association of 13 a-priori JAK-STAT genes with ENIGMA MDD cortical-thickness effect sizes; (ii) matched random-gene-set and competitive gene-set tests; (iii) sensitivity to cortical co-expression gradients, quantified with Bayes factors; (iv) cell-type attribution; (v) cis-Mendelian randomization of *IL6R* against depression and cortical thickness — a design with no spatial structure at all; (vi) postmortem brain differential expression with an explicit positive control; and (vii) prospective power analysis. We report convergent null evidence across all layers, show that the nominal spatial signal is fully attributable to the cortical principal gradient, and provide a minimum-reporting framework for imaging-transcriptomic enrichment claims.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Study design and data sources

All analyses used publicly available summary-level or processed data; no new human participants were involved and no additional ethics approval was required. Data sources are summarised in Table 1.

### 2.2 Spatial transcriptomic association

Thirteen JAK-STAT genes were specified a priori: *IL6R, IL6ST, JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, TYK2, STAT1, STAT3, STAT4, STAT5A, STAT5B, STAT6, SOCS1*. Regional expression (Schaefer-400) was related to regional cortical-thickness Cohen's *d* using Spearman correlation across *n* = 339 regions with complete data. Because a correlation with signed effect sizes can be driven by their sign structure, we report the five-step sign diagnostic of Chen & colleagues [**补引用**]: correlation with *d*, correlation with |*d|*, and the per-gene pattern of these two coefficients. Spatial autocorrelation was addressed with the spin test (Freedman–Perry; 10,000 rotations) [28].

### 2.3 Null models (matched random gene sets and competitive gene sets)

To ask whether the JAK-STAT gene set carries spatial information beyond that of comparable genes, we drew 10,000 random gene sets matched on three properties: mean brain expression level, gene length, and SNP count (a proxy for LD coverage) [**补：匹配卡尺/算法**]. Three pre-specified summary statistics were evaluated: (a) the number of genes reaching nominal *P* < 0.05 and the observed/expected ratio; (b) the gene-set mean |ρ|; (c) the sum of |ρ| across the 18 highest-|ρ| gene × phenotype grid cells. Empirical two-sided *P* values were computed against the null distribution. The formal competitive gene-set test was performed in MAGMA (one-sided upper-tail Z > 1.645).

### 2.4 Sensitivity to cortical co-expression gradients

The full-gene Schaefer-400 expression matrix was decomposed by singular value decomposition; PC1 (13.9% of variance) indexes the sensory-to-association axis. We computed partial correlations ρ(gene, *d* | PC1) and ρ(gene, *d* | PC1–PC5). The pre-specified criterion for "gradient-driven" was: ≥80% attenuation of |ρ| *and* *P* > 0.05 after gradient control. As a reference, we report the correlation of PC1 itself with *d*.

### 2.5 Bayes factors

Bayes factors (BFs) were computed to distinguish evidence *for* the null from mere non-significance [29,30]. For correlations and two-sample *t*-tests we report the JZS default-prior BF (Rouder et al., *r* = 0.707, numerical integration) and the BIC approximation as a sensitivity check [30,31]. For genetic associations (MR and GWAS summary statistics) we used Wakefield's approximate Bayes factor with prior variance W swept over clinically plausible ranges (effect-size priors stated per analysis) [32]. Evidence categories follow Jeffreys: BF₀₁ > 3 moderate, > 10 strong for the null.

### 2.6 Cell-type attribution

Regional expression of the 13 genes was correlated (Spearman, *n* = 339 regions) with eight cell-class marker-panel scores (microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, OPCs, endothelium, pericytes, excitatory and inhibitory neurons), each score being the mean across-region z of its marker genes [26]. Panel inter-correlations are reported as a discriminant check. Region-level covariation does not constitute single-cell evidence; this limitation is retained in the interpretation.

### 2.7 Genetic tests and cis-Mendelian randomization

The competitive MAGMA test (2.3) provides the pathway-level genetic test. For causal inference we used rs2228145 (Asp358Ala) as a cis instrument for IL6R signalling: effect allele C, EAF 0.39, explaining ≈51% of plasma sIL-6R variance (β = 1.035 SD per C allele) [33]. The Wald ratio θ = β_out/β_exp with SE = SE_out/|β_exp| gives the causal estimate per SD sIL-6R; normal-approximation *P* values are reported, together with sensitivity analyses across the published range of β_exp (0.49–1.035). Outcomes: (a) PGC MDD 2018 (log-odds) [22]; (b) global mean cortical thickness (GCST90271815, *n* = 32,488) [23]; (c) a pre-specified panel of 12 regional thickness measures and total surface area [24]. The global mean was the pre-specified primary endpoint; regional analyses are supportive descriptions. Stouffer *Z* across regions is reported as an independence upper bound only, because regional estimates are correlated; the primary inference is the global endpoint. Two non-significant outcomes (thickness vs surface area) were not contrasted to claim specificity. Statistical power is expressed as the minimum detectable effect (MDE) at 80% power, two-sided α = 0.05.

### 2.8 Postmortem brain differential expression

We analysed GSE53987 (Affymetrix HG-U133 Plus 2; 205 arrays; dorsolateral prefrontal cortex [BA46], hippocampus, striatum; schizophrenia [SCZ] 48, bipolar disorder 52, MDD 50, healthy controls 55; 15–19 per region × group) [25]. The submitted matrix is on a linear intensity scale (observed range 4.59–25,856) and was log2-transformed before testing. Multi-probe resolution used a pre-specified rule: the probe with the highest mean expression across all samples represents the gene (all probes are reported in Supplementary S1); probes were never selected by *P* value. Gene-level tests used Welch's *t* and Mann–Whitney *U*, and covariate-adjusted ordinary least squares (expression ~ group + age + gender + PMI + pH + RIN). Correction was by Benjamini–Hochberg FDR within region × contrast across 13 genes (FDR₁₃) and across all 13 × 3 = 39 tests for the primary MDD comparison (FDR₃₉). Groups were well balanced on age (45.5–48.2 y), PMI (19.4–20.5 h), pH (6.47–6.61), RIN (7.39–7.81) and sex (52.7–56.0% male). The identical pipeline applied to SCZ vs controls served as the positive control for assay sensitivity; this cohort has been reported to show widespread inflammatory transcriptional increases in SCZ but not in MDD [25]. Power was expressed as the MDE in fold-change.

### 2.9 Reporting

All scripts, seeds and result tables are public [**补 GitHub URL**]. Empirical *P* values are two-sided. No exploratory subgroups were tested.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 A strong nominal spatial association — and its sign structure

Expression of JAK-STAT genes correlated positively with regional cortical-thickness effect sizes (IL6R: ρ = +0.315, *P* = 3.1 × 10⁻⁹; BF₁₀ > 10⁶, decisive). The association was specific to signed effect sizes: ρ with |*d*| = −0.267, and all 13 genes shared this sign pattern (ρ(*d*) > 0 with ρ(|*d*|) < 0). Regions of higher JAK-STAT expression were thus regions of relative preservation — a plausible-looking, highly significant, directionally coherent pattern.

### 3.2 The association is not distinguishable from matched random gene sets

Against 10,000 gene sets matched on brain expression level, gene length and SNP count, the JAK-STAT set was unremarkable (Table 2).

| Statistic | Observed | Matched null (mean ± SD) | Empirical *P* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genes at nominal *P* < 0.05 | 3/13 (*TYK2* 0.005, *STAT5A* 0.034, *STAT1* 0.043); O/E = 4.62 | 1.23 ± 1.04 | **0.116** |
| Gene-set mean \|ρ\| | 0.137 | 0.204 ± 0.031 | **0.985** |
| Top-18 grid Σ\|ρ\| | 5.44 | 6.99 ± 0.76 | **0.978** |
| MAGMA competitive gene-set | — | — | **0.744** |

The apparent 4.6-fold excess of nominal hits is not significant against matched random sets (*P* = 0.116), and the gene set's spatial signal is *lower* than the matched null average (*P* = 0.985).

### 3.3 The association is absorbed by the cortical principal gradient

PC1 of the whole-brain expression matrix correlated with the imaging map at ρ = +0.4907 (*P* = 6.1 × 10⁻²²) — stronger than any single gene. Gradient control dissolved the gene-level associations (Table 3, Fig. 1).

| Gene | ρ(*d*) | Partial ρ \| PC1 | Partial ρ \| PC1–PC5 | Attenuation | BF₀₁ (PC1–5) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *IL6R* | +0.315 | +0.179 | **+0.005** (*P* = 0.93) | **98.5%** | **16.4** |
| Remaining 12 genes | +0.25 to +0.32 | — | all \|ρ\| < 0.02 | ≥ 90% | — |

After controlling PC1 alone the association retained an anecdotal BF₁₀ ≈ 14; after PC1–PC5 the Bayes factor reversed to **BF₀₁ = 16.4 (strong evidence for the null)**. The pre-specified "gradient-driven" criterion (≥80% attenuation, *P* > 0.05) was met by all 13 genes.

### 3.4 Cell-type attribution contradicts a microglial mechanism

IL6R regional expression tracked the endothelial panel (ρ = +0.39) and not the microglial panel (ρ = −0.14); *STAT4* tracked excitatory neurons (ρ = +0.57, microglia −0.40); *STAT3* tracked oligodendrocytes (ρ = +0.34). Microglial correlations for all 13 genes were negative or near zero. Panel inter-correlations were as expected (microglia vs excitatory neurons *r* = −0.46; endothelium vs pericytes *r* = 0.55), supporting discriminant validity. This is consistent with vascular/endothelial IL6R biology and inconsistent with a microglial narrative.

### 3.5 Genetic evidence is null, with limited precision

The competitive MAGMA test was negative (*P* = 0.744). In PGC MDD 2018, rs2228145 (A allele) showed no association with depression (OR = 0.989, *P* = 0.230), nor did four neighbouring *IL6R* variants. Wakefield Bayes factors were inconclusive to anecdotal for the null (BF₀₁ = 1.3–5.3 across effect-size priors W = 0.02–0.10 on the log-odds scale).

For the pre-specified primary endpoint — global mean cortical thickness — cis-MR of IL6R signalling gave β = −0.0119 SD per C allele (Wald θ = −0.0115, SE = 0.0077, *z* = −1.49, **95% CI −0.0267 to +0.0036, *P* = 0.135**): a null. Wakefield BF₀₁ ranged 0.81–3.34 across prior SDs of 0.01–0.08 SD/allele — inconclusive to anecdotal for the null, consistent with the observed effect being 53% of the MDE (0.0224 SD/allele).

Across the 12 regional thickness measures, estimates were uniformly negative (12/12; 5/12 nominally significant; none survived Bonferroni α = 0.0042; strongest *P* = 0.0086). Stouffer *Z* = −5.25 (independence upper bound; *P* = 1.5 × 10⁻⁷) — because regions are correlated, the true panel significance lies between this bound and the global *P* = 0.135, and the global endpoint is the conservative reference. The largest regional estimate (ventral frontal, β = −0.0203, 95% CI −0.0354 to −0.0051) reached 91% of the MDE and showed anecdotal-to-moderate evidence for a regionally concentrated effect (BF₁₀ ≈ 5–7 at generous priors), but this is an uncorrected observation among 12 correlated tests and is reported as description only. Total surface area was likewise null (β = +0.0111, *P* = 0.166); two non-significant outcomes were not contrasted to claim phenotype specificity.

### 3.6 No differential expression in MDD postmortem brain — with a positive control

Across 39 gene × region tests (13 genes × PFC/hippocampus/striatum), no gene reached FDR < 0.05 in MDD vs controls (Table 4, Fig. 2). Two tests were nominally significant (expectation 1.95); the smallest *P* was 0.027 (*STAT1*/PFC; FDR₃₉ = 0.752); the largest absolute change was 0.139 log₂ = 1.10-fold. Gene-set direction was inconsistent (PFC 6 up / 7 down, sign-test *P* = 1.00; hippocampus 4/9, *P* = 0.27; striatum 6/7, *P* = 1.00), and a 13-gene panel *z*-test was negative in all regions (*P* = 0.97/0.45/0.83). IL6R changed in opposite directions across regions (PFC −0.137 vs striatum +0.132), incompatible with coherent pathway activation. JZS Bayes factors gave a median BF₀₁ = 4.8 (moderate evidence for the null; 26/39 ≥ 3; none ≥ 10, the sample-size ceiling); the largest BF₁₀ was 1.82 (*STAT1*/PFC) — anecdotal at best.

The identical pipeline applied to schizophrenia recovered three FDR-significant genes: *IL6ST* (PFC, +0.256, *P* = 0.00099, FDR₃₉ = 0.038, BF₁₀ = 33), *STAT3* (PFC, +0.222, *P* = 0.0036, FDR₃₉ = 0.047, BF₁₀ = 13) and *JAK1* (hippocampus, −0.289, *P* = 0.0031, FDR₃₉ = 0.047, BF₁₀ = 14), with 9/39 nominally significant; covariate-adjusted models retained one (IL6ST/PFC, *P* = 0.0088). Bipolar disorder showed 0/39. These schizophrenia findings are consistent with the original report from this cohort describing widespread inflammatory transcriptional increases in schizophrenia but not in MDD [25]; they are reported here as a positive control establishing assay sensitivity, not as novel observations.

Power analysis located the interpretive boundary: the MDE was 1.12/1.16/1.12-fold (PFC/hippocampus/striatum), and the largest observed change (1.10-fold) fell below it. Effects larger than ≈1.1-fold can therefore be excluded in this cohort; smaller effects cannot, and the Bayes factors reflect exactly this precision.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal finding

Across seven complementary tests, JAK-STAT signalling showed no convergent association with MDD-related cortical alterations. The single nominally positive result — the spatial correlation between JAK-STAT gene expression and cortical-thickness effect sizes — was not distinguishable from matched random gene sets, was negative by competitive gene-set testing, and was fully absorbed by cortical co-expression gradients (BF₀₁ = 16.4 after PC1–PC5). Independent genetic and postmortem transcriptional lines concurred, with a positive control (schizophrenia) establishing assay sensitivity.

### 4.2 Why the illusion is persuasive

The value of this case is not the null itself but the explanation of why a null can look so convincingly positive. Three mechanisms, all directly quantified here, act together: (i) a **gradient prior** — the cortical principal gradient correlates with the imaging map at ρ = 0.49, stronger than any single gene, so any gene expressed along the sensory-to-association axis will "match" an MDD thickness map; (ii) **co-expression** — arbitrary matched gene sets show equal or greater spatial signal (empirical *P* = 0.985), so spatial enrichment is a generic property of gene sets, not a pathway signature; and (iii) **nominal excess** — 3 of 13 hits reads as a 4.6-fold excess until compared with matched sets (*P* = 0.116). This extends the EEG-genetics demonstration that spatial-null correction is "necessary but not sufficient" [18] by supplying the mechanism (the gradient) and a major psychiatric phenotype, and it quantifies the illusion with Bayes factors: a decisive BF₁₀ > 10⁶ before gradient control becomes BF₀₁ = 16.4 after.

### 4.3 What the null does and does not say

Semantic boundaries matter. This study does **not** refute peripheral IL-6 elevation in depression, animal IL-6/STAT3→behaviour work, or the rationale for JAK inhibition in inflammation-related anhedonia [1–6] — those are peripheral, state-marker and interventional questions. Nor does it show that "JAK-STAT is unrelated to MDD" in every sense. It shows that three specific, widely used forms of human brain evidence — imaging-transcriptomic spatial convergence, cis-genetic instruments for IL6R, and postmortem brain differential expression — provide no support, and that the spatial form is explained by cortical architecture. A defensible summary is: *peripheral activation is not evidence of central transcriptional mechanism, and imaging-transcriptomic pathway stories require stronger null models than are currently standard.* The estimate boundaries are explicit: postmortem changes larger than ≈1.10-fold can be excluded; MR effects larger than 0.022 SD/allele can be excluded; smaller effects remain below the resolution of these data.

### 4.4 Positive-control logic

The schizophrenia results are the internal-validity anchor of the postmortem analysis: the same matrix, probes and statistics recover IL6ST/STAT3/JAK1 in SCZ but nothing in MDD, so the MDD null is not a technical failure. We emphasise that these SCZ findings replicate the original report from this cohort [25] and are offered as a positive control only.

### 4.5 A minimum-reporting framework

We suggest that imaging-transcriptomic enrichment claims be accompanied by: (1) a spatial autocorrelation-preserving null *and* a matched gene-set null (expression level × gene length × LD/SNP count); (2) sensitivity to co-expression gradients (PC1–PC5 or equivalent); (3) competitive gene-set testing reported separately from gene-level results; (4) cell-type attribution with its region-level limitation declared; (5) at least one independent line of evidence with no spatial structure (GWAS/MR/postmortem); (6) positive controls and prospective power/MDE; and (7) public code and full result tables. Items (1)–(3) directly address the three mechanisms identified in 4.2. This checklist is compatible with and extends recent AHBA reporting guidance [**补 2026 checklist 引用**].

### 4.6 Limitations

First, the postmortem analysis is a single platform and cohort (GSE53987), with an MDE of 1.12-fold; smaller effects are not excluded. Second, cis-MR relies on one instrument (rs2228145); horizontal pleiotropy cannot be fully excluded, and multi-instrument IVW awaits published sIL-6R coefficients for rs4845618/rs4129267. Third, AHBA comprises six adult donors without MDD, and results depend on Schaefer-400 parcellation. Fourth, cell-type attribution is region-level covariation, not single-cell evidence. Fifth, ENIGMA effect-size maps are group-level summaries and regions are not independent; we therefore treat Stouffer *Z* as an upper bound and the global endpoint as primary. Sixth, the 13-gene panel is a priori and does not span the full JAK-STAT family (SOCS1 only among the SOCS genes). Finally, Bayes factors inherit their prior; we report prior sweeps for the genetic analyses and dual estimators for the frequentist-scale tests.

### 4.7 Conclusion

Imaging-transcriptomic enrichment claims require spatial nulls *and* gene-set nulls *and* gradient-sensitivity checks before they can be read as molecular evidence. Applied to the JAK-STAT hypothesis in MDD, these checks reduce a nominally strong spatial convergence to noise, and independent genetic and postmortem lines concur. The framework is directly reusable for the many imaging-transcriptomic studies now reporting pathway-level conclusions in psychiatry.
